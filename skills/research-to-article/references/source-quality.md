# Source Quality and Article Evidence

Choose sources by directness, authority, recency, and relevance to the exact claim. A lower-ranked source can still be useful when it is the original evidence for a specific observation.

## Evidence Roles

- `fact`: directly stated or measured by the cited evidence
- `analysis`: the writer's interpretation of supported facts
- `inference`: a reasonable conclusion not directly stated by the source
- `scenario`: an illustrative possibility, not a reported event
- `recommendation`: advice derived from evidence and stated assumptions

## Source Priority

1. Official documentation, records, standards, datasets, filings, or first-party announcements.
2. Original research, papers, experiments, interviews, transcripts, or direct creator material.
3. Credible secondary reporting with named evidence and clear dates.
4. Expert analysis that exposes assumptions and links to underlying material.
5. Aggregators, newsletters, forums, and social posts used as discovery leads unless they are the primary object of analysis.

## Date Handling

- Record the date a source was published and the date the event occurred when they differ.
- Compare claims against the article's evidence cutoff date.
- Use dated wording for counts, roles, availability, pricing, policies, and product capabilities that may change.
- Do not use a newer article as proof that an older event happened on the newer publication date.

## Conflicts and Gaps

- Prefer the most direct current source when definitions and scope match.
- If definitions differ, explain the difference instead of selecting a convenient number.
- If no source resolves the conflict, qualify or omit the claim.
- Never cite one source for a neighboring claim it does not support.

## Citation Discipline

Link to the page that supports the claim, not a search result. Keep quotations short. Credit creators, datasets, images, videos, and frameworks when they materially contribute to the article.
